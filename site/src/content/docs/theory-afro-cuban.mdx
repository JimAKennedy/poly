## The System

Cuban popular music is governed by **clave**: a five-stroke, two-bar (16-pulse) pattern that functions as a matrix rather than a part <sup>[10](/appendix-references/#ref-10)</sup>. Peñalosa's synthesis of Cuban pedagogy states the core principle: every part in the ensemble is aligned with clave, whether or not clave is being played aloud — alignment is what makes a part *correct*, and misalignment ("crossed", *cruzado*) is a recognised, nameable error, not a stylistic choice ([Peñalosa 2009](/appendix-references/#fr-penalosa-2009)).

The matrix has two halves with opposite characters:

- **The three-side** (the bar containing three strokes) is the *offbeat* side — generative, tense, forward-leaning. Its three strokes are the tresillo skeleton, and two of them have names because parts target them: the **bombo** (second stroke, pulse 3 of 0–15) and the **ponche** (third stroke, pulse 6).
- **The two-side** (the bar with two strokes) is the *onbeat* side — stable, grounded, resolving. Its strokes sit on or beside the main beats.

A groove is "in 3-2" or "2-3" depending on which side the ensemble treats as bar one. Direction is a property of the *whole arrangement*: melody, bass, percussion all imply the same direction, and one part implying the opposite direction is what "crossed" means precisely.

## The Rules

1. **Declare the clave and its direction first.** Everything else is defined against it. The clave itself never varies — it is the referent, and like the Ewe bell it takes no ornamentation, no dynamics contour, no mutation. (Note the exact son and rumba claves are *non-Euclidean*; use the timeline-mode workflow from [Chapter 3](/03-afro-cuban/) to get the true pattern.)

2. **Match tension to sides.** Parts place their offbeat, syncopated activity on the three-side and their grounded, onbeat activity on the two-side. This is the master alignment rule: a part whose busy half sits against the two-side implies the reversed clave and is crossed ([Peñalosa 2009](/appendix-references/#fr-penalosa-2009); [Mauleón 1993](/appendix-references/#fr-mauleon-1993)).

3. **The bass tumbao avoids the downbeat and targets bombo and ponche.** The classic son bass sounds on the bombo (pulse 3) and ponche (pulse 6) of the three-side and sustains across bar lines. Beat-one bass is not neutral — it audibly shifts the style toward son montuno's older *danzón* ancestors or toward non-Cuban pop ([Mauleón 1993](/appendix-references/#fr-mauleon-1993)).

4. **Each percussion voice has an archetype part, and the archetype is clave-locked.** Cáscara/palito patterns, the timbale bell, the bongó *martillo*, the conga *marcha* (tumbao) are fixed patterns with defined clave alignment — learned as wholes, varied only in sanctioned ways ([Uribe 1996](/appendix-references/#fr-uribe-1996)). The conga marcha places its paired open tones in the second half of each bar, leading across the barline; the quinto (in rumba) is the designated free voice.

5. **The variation budget belongs to one voice at a time.** In son: the bongosero moves between martillo and bell, and improvised *repiques* are momentary. In rumba: the quinto improvises continuously while salidor and tres golpes hold their parts. Either way, the low parts and the clave never join the improvisation.

6. **Density complements across parts, not within them.** The composite of clave + cáscara + tumbao + marcha covers most of the 16 pulses with almost no doubled strong accents — parts interlock around each other's strokes (the cinquillo fills the tresillo's gaps). When adding a lane, place its accents in composite gaps, not on existing accents.

7. **The feel is a lilt, not straight and not swung.** Cuban subdivision sits between straight sixteenths and triplets — roughly 0.2–0.3 on Poly's Swing parameter — applied ensemble-wide with per-part micro-differences. (See <sup>[39](/appendix-references/#ref-39)</sup> for measured groove-genre timing.)

8. **Crossing is a deliberate, temporary device or an error — nothing in between.** Modern timba deliberately "crosses" for sections (*gears*, bomba sections) and resolves back; what it never does is drift ambiguously. If a part contradicts the matrix, it should be obvious, sectional, and resolved ([Washburne 1997](/appendix-references/#fr-washburne-1997) traces the same logic into jazz).

## Construction

1. **Clave lane.** 16 steps at 1/16, timeline mode with the exact son clave (hits 0, 3, 6, 10, 12 for 3-2). Decide the direction now (rotate for 2-3).
2. **Bass/tumbao lane.** Hits at bombo and ponche positions; long note durations; no beat-one hit (Rule 3).
3. **Cáscara lane.** The shell pattern, its dense half against the three-side (Rule 2).
4. **Marcha lane.** Conga, mid density, open-tone pair placed late in each bar (Rule 4), ghost floor for the filler strokes between accents.
5. **One free voice.** Quinto-like lane holding the mutation budget (Rule 5), ideally with fills weighted toward the two-side's space so answers land against the stable half.
6. **Feel.** Swing 0.2–0.3 ensemble-wide, slight per-lane offsets (Rule 7). Check the composite for doubled accents (Rule 6) and for any lane whose busy half fights the declared direction (Rule 2).

## What Breaks the Idiom

- **The approximate clave.** E(5,16) is bossa's pattern, not Cuba's. Shipping it as "clave" is the most common generator error; the fifth hit's position (13 vs 12) changes the two-side's meaning.
- **Crossed parts.** A lane whose accent pattern implies the opposite direction — most often a two-bar part built without deciding which bar is which. Test: mute everything but clave and the suspect lane; if the busy half sits on the two-side, it's crossed (Rule 2).
- **Beat-one bass.** Instantly un-Cuban (Rule 3).
- **A second free voice.** Two lanes improvising simultaneously dissolves the matrix's figure/ground relationship (Rule 5).
- **Straight-grid feel or jazz-triplet swing.** Both miss the 0.2–0.3 zone (Rule 7).
