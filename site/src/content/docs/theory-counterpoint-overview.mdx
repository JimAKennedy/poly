The chapters of this guide are deliberately broad and shallow: each one introduces a tradition, shows its signature patterns, and gets you to a playing patch quickly. The theory deep dives in this section are the opposite — narrow and deep. Each one takes a single tradition and states, as explicitly as we can manage, the *rules* that make a multi-lane texture idiomatic in that tradition: what must stay fixed, what may vary and by how much, which relationships between voices are structural and which are decorative, and what specifically breaks the idiom.

Three things to be clear about before you dive in.

**None of this is original research.** Every deep dive is a summary of published scholarship — ethnomusicological fieldwork, performer accounts, measured timing studies — restated in Poly's vocabulary and compressed into usable rules. The pages cite their sources inline, list them at the bottom, and each carries an Attribution note distinguishing rules taken directly from the literature from rules that are this guide's practical distillation of documented practice. The intellectual credit for the analysis belongs to the scholars and musicians cited; what this section adds is selection, arrangement, and the mapping onto Poly's parameters.

**These are models, not the music.** Every rule set here is a compression of a living practice, written from the scholarship cited at the bottom of each page. A rule like "the timeline never varies" is true the way a grammar rule is true — real masters bend these rules knowingly, and the traditions themselves vary by region, era, and ensemble. Treat the deep dives as a rigorous starting point, follow the citations when a rule surprises you, and listen to the source music above all.

**Validity and idiom are different tests.** Poly's engine guarantees *validity*: every patch is playable, deterministic, and internally consistent. It does not guarantee *idiom* — the engine will happily let you swing an aksak cycle or mutate a clave. These pages define the idiom layer: the constraints a knowledgeable musician applies on top of what the machine allows.

## The Shared Framework

Reading the eleven deep dives side by side, the same five structural questions organise every tradition. Each page answers them for its style; learning to ask them is the transferable skill.

The framework itself is an organising device for this guide, not a new theory: the *referent* concept is [Arom's](/appendix-references/#fr-arom-1991) (developed for Central African polyrhythm and long applied to timeline musics generally), the cross-tradition comparability of timeline patterns is argued by [Pressing 2002](/appendix-references/#fr-pressing-2002) and Toussaint <sup>[1](/appendix-references/#ref-1)</sup>, and the remaining questions name distinctions that recur throughout the cited literature. We have arranged existing ideas; we have not discovered them.

### 1. The referent

Every tradition has a **referent**: a fixed part that everything else is heard against. The Ewe gankogui bell, the Cuban clave, the tabla theka, the jazz ride pattern, the techno four-on-the-floor kick, the samba surdo pair — functionally, these are the same object. The referent is not necessarily loud, and it is not always literally played (Cuban musicians *feel* clave whether or not anyone strikes it), but every other part is defined by its relationship to it.

**In Poly:** identify the referent lane first, lock it (timeline mode, zero mutation, no phrase gating), and configure every other lane *relative* to it. A patch without a designated referent is the single most common cause of unidiomatic output.

### 2. Complementarity

Idiomatic parts occupy the spaces other parts leave open — by filling a referent's gaps (Balinese kotekan, Ewe support drums), by answering across a cycle (call-and-response), or by claiming a distinct subdivision stratum (Tony Allen's kick/hat/snare separation). Random overlap is the sound of a machine; structured complementarity is the sound of an ensemble.

**In Poly:** the Kotekan parameter automates strict complementation; rotation, step-count choice, and phrase gating handle the looser forms.

### 3. Role hierarchy and the variation budget

Every ensemble distributes a fixed *variation budget* unevenly: the referent gets none, support parts get sanctioned micro-variations, and one designated voice (lead drum, jazz comping, conga quinto) gets nearly all of it. Traditions differ enormously in *where* the budget sits — funk repeats almost everything and jazz varies almost everything — but no tradition distributes it uniformly.

**In Poly:** mutation, probability, and complexity-sensitivity are the budget. Concentrate them on one or two lanes; zero them on the referent.

### 4. Convergence and cadence

Independent lines must agree somewhere, and where they agree is structural: the Ewe cycle downbeat, tala's *sam*, the LCM convergence of phasing patterns, the four-bar phrase boundary of a jazz form. Idiomatic counterpoint doesn't just avoid the convergence point being random — it *aims* at it: fills, tihais, and lead phrases cadence there.

**In Poly:** convergence emerges from cycle-length arithmetic (see the [Timing Model](/appendix-timing-model/)); phrase gating and envelope periods let you make it a target rather than an accident.

### 5. The feel layer

Beneath the pattern layer, every tradition has a *systematic* micro-timing and dynamics profile — samba's long-short-short-long sixteenths, jazz's tempo-dependent swing ratio, funk's push-pull pocket, the deliberate flatness of techno. Two facts matter: the profiles are consistent (not random jitter — see [Hennig et al. 2011](/appendix-references/#fr-hennig-2011)), and they are not interchangeable (applying swing to aksak breaks it).

**In Poly:** per-lane swing, timing offset, per-step micro-timing, ghost floor, spread, and the Tension macro implement this layer. Each deep dive states the target profile.

## A Note on Sources and Respect

The rules on these pages are drawn from published scholarship and pedagogy — ethnomusicological fieldwork, performer autobiographies, measured timing studies — cited inline and collected in the [References appendix](/appendix-references/). Where scholars disagree, or where a "rule" is really one region's practice, the pages say so. None of this substitutes for listening to, learning from, and crediting the musicians who carry these traditions; the deep dives exist to make your borrowings informed rather than accidental.
