## The System

Gamelan offers two distinct counterpoint systems that Poly models separately. **Balinese kotekan** is micro-level interlock: two parts (*polos* and *sangsih*) divide a rapid figuration between them so that their composite is a continuous stream neither player could sustain alone ([Vitale 1990](/appendix-references/#fr-vitale-1990); [Tenzer 2000](/appendix-references/#fr-tenzer-2000)). **Javanese colotomic structure** is macro-level nesting: instruments mark points in a long cycle at stroke rates related by powers of two, producing a temporal hierarchy in which the largest gong subsumes all smaller cycles ([Sumarsam 1995](/appendix-references/#fr-sumarsam-1995)) <sup>[18](/appendix-references/#ref-18), [19](/appendix-references/#ref-19)</sup>.

Both systems share one deep property: the cycle is **end-weighted**. The gong stroke and the kotekan cadence tone fall on the *completion* of the cycle, which is simultaneously the start of the next — arrival, not departure. This inverts the Western downbeat instinct and governs where every part places its weight.

## The Rules

### Kotekan (Bali)

1. **The composite must be continuous.** At the interlock's subdivision rate, (almost) every pulse is struck by exactly one of the pair. Gaps in the composite are errors; the whole point is a stream faster than one player's hand.

2. **Each part alone must be playable and idiomatic.** Parts avoid long same-hand runs at maximum speed; each is a sparse, syncopated figure with its own kinetic logic. If one "part" of your pair is an unplayable near-continuous stream, you have written a melody plus an echo, not kotekan.

3. **Polos leans onbeat, sangsih leans offbeat.** The division of labour is not arbitrary: polos ("basic") carries more of the beat-coincident strokes; sangsih ("differing") carries the off-positions and the higher notes where the parts differ in pitch ([Vitale 1990](/appendix-references/#fr-vitale-1990)).

4. **The parts overlap at structural tones.** Strict complementation is only the textbook case. In practice both players strike together at cadence points, phrase joins, and *angsel* (break) figures — the overlap marks structure ([Tenzer 2000](/appendix-references/#fr-tenzer-2000)). A pair with empty intersection everywhere sounds mechanical. (Poly's Kotekan `L1` implements the strict case; add deliberate doublings via a third lane or accent masks until kotekan modes ship.)

5. **The style of interlock is a named choice.** *Norot* oscillates around neighbouring tones; *kotekan telu* shares three-pulse cells; *kotekan empat* four; *nyog cag* alternates strictly. Each has a different composite texture — choose one and keep it; mixing interlock styles mid-phrase is not idiomatic.

6. **The interlock serves a melody.** Kotekan elaborates a slower core melody (*pokok*). Its accents must land on the pokok tones at the pokok's rate — the fast surface is an ornamentation system, not free figuration.

### Colotomy (Java, and the Balinese gong cycle)

7. **Stroke rates halve as instruments deepen.** If the ketuk marks every unit, the kempul marks every 2, kenong every 4, gong every 8 (or deeper). Ratios are powers of two; a 3:1 layer belongs to a different system.

8. **Weight accrues to coincidence, and the gong takes all.** The more layers coincide on a stroke, the heavier it is; the gong stroke — where everything coincides — is the heaviest event and falls at the cycle's *end/beginning*. Nothing competes with it: texture typically thins just before it and re-blooms after.

9. **Density scales with register, inversely.** The lowest instruments play slowest, the highest fastest (the kotekan sits at the top of this pyramid). A low, fast voice or a high, slow voice inverts the pyramid and reads as foreign.

10. **Tempo and density trade off (irama).** When the beat slows, elaborating parts double their subdivision so surface density stays roughly constant. In Poly: halving tempo should be paired with doubling elaborating lanes' step counts or subdivision — the surface stream is the invariant.

## Construction

1. **Colotomic frame.** Four lanes, one hit each, steps 4/8/16/32 at 1/4 (Rule 7), velocity increasing with depth (Rule 8), rotations aligned so all coincide at the cycle boundary.
2. **Pokok.** A mid-register lane, 8 steps, 4–8 hits, even and calm (Rule 6's melody proxy).
3. **Kotekan pair.** Polos: 8 or 16 steps, 5 hits, biased onbeat (Rule 3). Sangsih: Kotekan `L{polos}` for the strict complement (Rule 1). Check polos alone for playability (Rule 2).
4. **Structural overlap.** Add doubled strokes at the cycle boundary — accent masks on both pair lanes, or a sparse third lane striking with both at the gong point (Rule 4).
5. **Feel.** No swing, no humanize: gamelan ensembles prize unison precision. Dynamics terraced by register, not contoured within parts.

## What Breaks the Idiom

- **Gaps in the kotekan composite** (Rule 1) — usually caused by editing polos after setting the complement; re-derive sangsih after any polos change.
- **An unplayable polos.** Dense polos + sparse sangsih is a melody with decoration, not interlock (Rule 2).
- **Front-weighted cycles.** Accenting pulse 0 as a Western downbeat instead of weighting the cycle's completion (Rule 8's end-weighting).
- **Non-binary colotomic ratios** (Rule 7) — a 3-layer belongs to the African/Cuban pages, not here.
- **Swing or humanize.** Gamelan's precision is the aesthetic; looseness reads as error, not warmth.
