## The System

Samba's ensemble is organised by three interlocking referents rather than one. The **surdo pair** supplies the metric floor as a *dialogue*: the first surdo answers on beat 2, the second marks beat 1 — and crucially, samba is **beat-two weighted**: the deep, sustained stroke falls on 2, inverting the Western downbeat instinct. Above it, the **tamborim's telecoteco** pattern functions as the timeline — Sandroni analyses it as samba's characteristic 16-pulse paradigm (his "Estácio paradigm"), a contratempo figure that took over from an older, more onbeat paradigm in the late 1920s ([Sandroni 2001](/appendix-references/#fr-sandroni-2001)). Underneath everything, the **caixa** supplies an unbroken sixteenth-note carpet with an internal accent pattern. Every other voice — agogô, repinique, chocalho, cuíca — is defined against these three strata ([Fryer 2000](/appendix-references/#fr-fryer-2000) traces the system's African ancestry).

The feel layer is unusually well measured: Brazilian sixteenths follow a systematic **long-short-short-long** timing profile with position-dependent amplitude, stable across players and tempi ([Gerischer 2006](/appendix-references/#fr-gerischer-2006); [Naveda et al. 2011](/appendix-references/#fr-naveda-2011)) — a signature that neither straight grids nor swing percentages reproduce.

## The Rules

1. **The surdo dialogue is fixed, and beat 2 is the heavy one.** Second surdo strikes 1 (often damped), first surdo answers 2 (open, ringing, louder). A third surdo, where present, adds syncopated pickup strokes into the pair. This alternation never varies during a section; it is the floor everyone dances on.

2. **The telecoteco is the timeline, and it is contratempo.** The tamborim/agogô stratum carries a fixed 16-pulse pattern that leans *off* the beats — its accents surround rather than strike the pulse ([Sandroni 2001](/appendix-references/#fr-sandroni-2001)). Like clave (its structural cousin), it has directionality: the pattern's syncopated and grounded halves must sit consistently against the two-bar sense, and parts derived from it inherit that orientation. It never mutates.

3. **The caixa never stops.** Continuous sixteenths, ghosted, with a repeating internal accent figure — the connective tissue that makes the ensemble roll. Gaps in the caixa stratum are section events (breaks commanded by the repinique), not texture.

4. **One voice commands.** The repinique (or the mestre's whistle) has the call role: entrances, breaks (*paradinhas*), and section changes are cued by its signal figures. Improvisation elsewhere is minimal — a batucada is a *machine of ostinatos* with one speaking voice, closer to funk's riff-lock than to jazz.

5. **Syncopation is around the beat, not against the meter.** Brazilian syncope displaces emphasis to the sixteenth *before or after* the beat (the characteristic tied-note figures), while the surdo floor keeps the beat itself unambiguous. Free polymeter over the samba grid is not idiomatic; all voices share the 16-pulse frame (same-grid layering, as in the Balkan and Manding models).

6. **The feel is long-short-short-long.** Within each beat's four sixteenths: first slightly long, middle two compressed, fourth slightly long again, with the profile's depth varying by instrument ([Gerischer 2006](/appendix-references/#fr-gerischer-2006); [Naveda et al. 2011](/appendix-references/#fr-naveda-2011)). It is a *timing* signature, mild enough that naive listeners call it "straight but alive." Swing parameters approximate it poorly (they displace only alternate notes); until subdivision profiles ship, use light swing (0.15–0.25) plus small per-lane offsets as an admitted approximation.

7. **Bossa is the system distilled, not simplified.** One displaced cell (the E(5,16) bass figure, or the guitar's thumb-and-fingers reduction of surdo + telecoteco) against an even pulse, at whisper dynamics. The rules survive the reduction: beat-2 weight in the thumb, contratempo lean in the upper voice, continuity in the ride/brush stratum. What disappears is mass, not structure.

## Construction

1. **Surdo floor.** One lane, 8 steps at 1/8, hits on beats 1 and 2 positions with velocity asymmetric — beat 2 stronger (Rule 1). (Two lanes at different pitches if you want the true pair.)
2. **Telecoteco stratum.** 16 steps at 1/16, timeline mode with the tamborim pattern (seed from E(5,16) or E(7,16) and adjust to the contratempo figure — the exact pattern varies by school; what matters is fixed, offbeat-leaning, never mutated) (Rule 2).
3. **Caixa carpet.** 16 steps, 13–16 hits, low velocity, ghost 50+, accent mask carrying the internal figure (Rule 3).
4. **Colour voices.** Agogô (E(5,16) rotated), chocalho (dense, quiet), each on the shared 16-grid (Rule 5).
5. **The commander.** Repinique lane with the mutation/fill budget and phrase gating for calls (Rule 4).
6. **Feel.** Swing 0.2 ± per-lane offsets as the Rule 6 approximation; Humanize ≤ 0.2.

## What Breaks the Idiom

- **Beat-one weighting.** The heaviest low stroke on 1 instead of 2 — instantly a march, not a samba (Rule 1).
- **A gappy caixa.** Thinning the carpet with density modulation removes the roll (Rule 3).
- **Distributed improvisation.** Mutation across many lanes; the batucada is ostinato-locked with one commander (Rule 4).
- **Polymetric layers.** Foreign cycle lengths against the 16-grid (Rule 5) — samba's evolution is the composite groove, not cycle divergence.
- **Deep swing.** 0.4+ swing turns the LSSL profile into a shuffle; Brazilian feel is shallower and position-shaped, not pair-shaped (Rule 6).
